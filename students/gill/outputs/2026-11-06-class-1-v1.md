## 1. The problem this class exists to solve

### 1.1 The succession problem

Vinnie's framing, and the reason the class exists at all, is the **succession problem**. In any organization of two people or more, somebody knows something, and then they go away, and the knowledge goes with them. His example is Steve Jobs: who comes after Steve Jobs? You have to train your replacement, but if the knowledge was never captured, there is no way to train them.

> "It is a problem that is as old as time, and now we have the AI tools, we have an opportunity to solve that problem, at least partially."
> [26:47]

The partial solution he proposes is a sequence: capture the knowledge from the people who are going away, store it, and transmit it by creating an adaptive learning program [26:47].

### 1.2 Vinnie's own version of the problem

The reason this is a class and not a product demo is that Vinnie has the same problem in his own work. He made a whole set of Runvil design decisions, at two a.m. on a Saturday morning. They are not written down. Nobody knows what they are. Consequently:

- no one can tell whether the code is doing the right thing;
- no one can tell whether an implementation is missing.

> "So your job is to get it out of me, and that is the same problem that the Knowledge Capture Corporation has."
> [18:39]

He describes the realisation that followed as the important moment of the session:

> "The problem that I am trying to solve is, how do I transfer that knowledge that I have over to you all. If we can solve that, now we are in business."
> [29:33]

This is the sentence that ties the class to the company. The class is not preparation for the work. The class is the work, at prototype scale.

### 1.3 The class is a rehearsal of the product

Vinnie is explicit that the team is **enacting the knowledge capture process**, not studying it:

- He participates in the capture by relating his stories of Runvil design [28:23].
- The team figures out how to capture that from the transcript and put it into a durable format in the repository [28:45].
- Their extraction prompts turn it into information the learning program can use [28:45].
- What the team learns about how they learn, and what works and does not work for them, is recorded, and that becomes the case study and the research behind the product [29:15].

> "We are enacting the knowledge capture process."
> [29:15]

The first deliverable does not have to be sophisticated. An instruction manual that people can simply read is useful; the interactive version, delivered through the pedagogue, comes later [28:45].

## 2. The context: what Runvil, Runvil Workshop, and Runvil Gateway are for

Three products carry the work. Vinnie introduces them by what each one refuses to concede to someone else.

### 2.1 Runvil is a prompting language: the author decides who has authority

The single design idea behind Runvil, in his words, is that **the author of the prompt decides which aspect has the authority**.

The default division is:

- **The model** starts out with the authority to perform inference.
- **The code** can be given the authority to orchestrate.

Everything else is opt-in. The author can:

- Delegate classification tasks to a **classifier**, because that is a feature Runvil will have to have, but is never forced to [12:05].
- Choose **where** model orchestration happens: throughout the entire prompt, or in part of the prompt. A prompt could open with Lua orchestration to do some research, hand the model a set of documents and internet links, and then let the model orchestrate from there [12:05].
- Offer tools **selectively**: give the model these tools in one section, and say no, you can only have these tools, in another [12:05].
- **Invent tools.** These are called **ad hoc tools**: you write one, offer it to the model, and the model can call it and do things [12:48].

> "The power of the language is that you have complete control."
> [12:05]

To understand what runs when a Runvil prompt runs, Vinnie points to one document: the tutorial *How an Agent Harness Works* [12:48], [13:13].

### 2.2 Runvil Workshop is an IDE: the author decides what context survives

Vinnie's stated reason for building an IDE at all is that he likes Cursor and never wants to return to the old way of doing things, does not want to run Visual Studio again, and does not want to switch IDEs. The problem is that he does not own Cursor, and its owners make decisions he does not agree with [13:47].

The specific complaint is architectural. In Cursor, a typed prompt goes to their server to enrich the context, comes back, and then goes to Anthropic. Vinnie does not want that, because he wants to enrich the content **his** way [13:47]. Different prompts need different enrichment, and different compaction policies:

- Sometimes I want some things to stay and some things to go when the agent compacts.
- It all depends on the prompt.
- Concretely: the mentograph might not need to keep a big history at all, only the last two stories. If so it could compact much more frequently and keep the token costs down [14:21].

> "Nobody is doing that, and this is an opportunity for us to do that."
> [14:21]

The IDE itself exists to have something that **understands Runvil** [14:51]. Concretely:

- Load a prompt, press a keyboard shortcut, and run it.
- Set a breakpoint at a spot, and then inspect: what is the state of this prompt, what does the store look like, what do the files look like, what did it do, what do the variables look like.
- Change something, and run it again.

The reason the third item matters is cost. Some of Vinnie's reporting tools take twenty or thirty minutes: they go on the internet, download web pages, look through them, and extract data. Paying that cost again on every edit is unacceptable [15:21].

So Runvil is designed to **record** a prompt run: every tool call, every function call in Lua, everything that happens, every interaction with the model. A replay of that recorded data puts the prompt back exactly where the breakpoint was, and from there you can try something different: change a constant, or change one prompt [15:46].

His worked example is a ten step pipeline under prompt optimisation. Steps one through six are good. Step seven hallucinates a little and needs fixing. Rerunning steps one through six could take half an hour, or two hours. What he wants is to start over at step seven with a different prompt and run that one step again on the same data [16:15].

Runvil Workshop will obviously be integrated with Runvil [16:46].

### 2.3 Runvil Gateway is a proxy: one protocol outward, each vendor's protocol inward

Gateway's problem is that **the inference endpoint has multiple shapes** [37:38].

- Today Gateway speaks the OpenAI shape: a generic inference endpoint where you present the messages and the tools, and it gives you the response.
- Getting the most from Claude, from ChatGPT, or from other frontier models requires their special API, which is a little bit different, so something special has to happen [37:58], [38:15].

Gateway's job, therefore, is to speak a generic protocol and translate it into whatever the vendor wants, and that requires a little cooperation from the Runvil engine [38:35]. The two cases Vinnie describes in detail are both about **thinking blocks that cannot be touched**:

- **Anthropic** attaches a cryptographic signature to its thinking blocks, so they cannot be tampered with, because someone who could tamper with the thinking could distil more effectively. A client must receive the hash attached to each thinking block and send it back. Nothing from the past may be edited, removed, or reordered: that breaks the chain, and breaking the chain kicks you out of the cache and returns an HTTP error. The request is not accepted [38:52], [39:08].
- **OpenAI / ChatGPT** is, in his word, worse: the thinking blocks are encrypted and redacted, so what comes back looks like random bits. OpenAI holds the key. The rule is the same: send it back as is, or the response is rejected [39:42].

There are also small differences in how each vendor handles tools [40:04].

The design constraint he gives Andrew Wheeler is a narrow one, and its narrowness is the point:

> Make our endpoint support the generic API and add just enough extensions that other apps who use the Gateway will not be bothered, but Runvil will be able to use minimal extensions, so that the Gateway can translate those requests into the proprietary API.
> [40:04], [40:22]

*Andrew Wheeler asked whether that internal protocol is already worked out, or still to be designed.* Answer: it would be in the design document; you have to look in the file - but it is not that complicated [40:50], [40:54].

### 2.4 The message stack, end to end

Vinnie's own walkthrough of a chat turn, which is the concrete picture *How an Agent Harness Works* is meant to give:

1. The agent takes the chat, the whole history, plus whatever you type in.
2. It packs that up and sends it to the **Gateway**.
3. The Gateway sends it out to **Claude**.
4. Claude calculates its response and sends it back to the Gateway.
5. The Gateway sends it to the **Workshop**.
6. It appears as additional chat.

There is a small extension that **streams** the results, so the words arrive as they are produced. Vinnie rejects the alternative outright: waiting for the whole thing to come in "would be lame"; the point is to see the words right away, for the instant gratification [41:58].

*Jason Brazeal pointed out that it is not only your message that gets sent; there is a whole background context, a system prompt, chat.md, saying things like "you are running in the Runvil harness."* Vinnie agreed: that is part of the messages, though he did not want to overwhelm the class with it on the first day [42:56].

His formulation, which is the one to teach from:

> "There is a stack of messages that Runvil maintains, and it grows every time you add something to it. When we want to talk to the model, we send that whole package to the model, and then it sends us the response back."
> [43:02]

Then the stack grows until it is too big, at which point "what I call the **AI lobotomy**" happens: it compacts, and you have room again [43:19].

In a Runvil prompt, that collection of messages is the thing called `messages` [43:19]. In the chat prompt, at the bottom, the user's input from the box is appended to `messages` by a **builder**, so `messages` grows, and then the **tool loop** runs and sends the new stack [43:58]. (The tool loop is explained in the tutorial document in the lessons.)

Then the detail that answers Andrew's protocol question at the level of data structures: on the Lua side there is this `messages` object, but it is backed by a **Rust struct** [44:22]. That struct can carry things Lua cannot see. You can add invisible fields to it - whatever you need in order to talk to the Gateway. For example, it carries the **tool catalog** [44:39].

## 3. What Vinnie is building: the Knowledge Capture Corporation

### 3.1 The company

The Knowledge Capture Corporation is a for-profit company with three products, all built on Runvil [11:08]. Vinnie states the position on profit plainly:

> "This is for profit, there is no nonprofit here, we are selling this, we are making money."
> [8:03]

The goal for the company is to get bought out or go public [11:08].

One boundary is permanent, and he states it as a correction to any assumption of enclosure:

> "Just to be clear, Runvil and Runvil Workshop will always be open source."
> [29:51]

The move from nonprofit to for-profit is, in his framing, caused by the class succeeding: if the knowledge transfer problem can be solved here, "now we are in business" [29:33].

### 3.2 Product 1: on-site knowledge capture

The customer is a company whose employees know things that are not written down. Vinnie's delivery options and mechanics:

- **Deployment**: a Docker container inside the customer's own network, or a cloud service. The commercial model is a subscription fee [5:50].
- **Interviews**: structured interviews, either human or AI assisted, with the employees who know things. They relate their experiences [5:50].
- **Transcripts**: the interviews turn into transcripts [5:50], [6:28].
- **Extraction**: the knowledge is extracted into **experiences and principles**, which could be design principles [6:28].
- **Storage**: those go into a database [6:28].
- **Delivery**: the database powers an interactive learning program [6:28].

### 3.3 Product 2: the autobiographical tool

Same technology, personal subject matter. Instead of talking about their business, the person talks about their life. The motivating cases Vinnie gives:

- Someone who is dying and wants to leave something for their grandchildren, so that they can know them [8:03].
- A retiring CEO whose stories about how he ran the company should be captured [8:03].

The most important design decision in this product is what it must **not** be:

> "The product that gets created here is not a virtual simulation of the person; that would be weird. Instead, it is a trusted virtual confidant."
> [8:43]

An AI persona who knows the person and can answer questions about them. His examples of the questions it should answer:

- "What was Vinnie like when he was up at Ripple?"
- "What was my grandfather like after World War II, when he was in the motor pool?"
- "What was Steve Jobs' approach to making new iPhones not suck?"

The last one is his illustration that there is corporate value in this product too [9:16].

Commercially this is a cloud service, automated: you go to the website, you do your interviews, it creates the persona, and you pay a monthly fee for access, or buy it and download the data [9:16].

### 3.4 Product 3: the knowledge marketplace

The familiar surface is deliberate. It looks just like YouTube: there is a thumbnail. The difference is what happens on click. Instead of a video, the learner gets whatever the author decided to make:

- an avatar with a voice,
- something that looks like a textbook,
- something that looks like a slideshow.

In every case it is a teaching program that teaches a subject, is interactive, and adapts to the learner [9:42]. It figures out how smart the learner is and how much they know, accelerates or slows down to their pace, and the author does not have to worry about any of that, because the system takes care of it [10:12].

The economics, in his terms:

- A subscription fee funds the marketplace [10:12].
- Creators are compensated with a fraction of the views, to incentivise them. The most popular learning programs are paid the most [10:12], [10:42].
- **Ten percent of all subscribers get to use ten percent of the learning programs for free, at random, provided they review afterwards and leave a comment** [10:42].

The last mechanism is worth understanding as a design, not a promotion. It exists so that ratings always arrive, and it cannot be gamed:

> "That guarantees that we always get ratings and that they are randomly distributed. It incentivises people to leave ratings, and they cannot be gamed because they are random."
> [10:42]

### 3.5 The pedagogue, and the teaching techniques inside it

The pedagogue is Vinnie's existing prototype: an interactive learning program that takes information and teaches it [5:16]. His assessment of his own prototypes is candid: "a little bit crude, but they are a proof of concept, and we can turn them into something real" [5:16].

What distinguishes it is that the prompt is **powered with actual methods that are effective in helping students learn** [6:53]. The rules visible in the prompt that he points to:

- **Never reveal the answer** [7:14].
- A set of **teaching rules** that the tutor follows when it is created [7:33].
- **Back off after repeated errors**: if a student makes two or three mistakes in a row, the tutor backs off, and maybe drops to a simpler topic so the student can build confidence [7:33].

The delivery mechanism he sketches for the richer version is a harness plus tool calls. A page that looks like a textbook, with text from the model, and a box with a multiple choice. The model issues a tool call that says, in his words, do a quick quiz "in order to move to the next section." If the learner fails, the adaptive program backs off, teaches more, and quizzes again. It could be graphical [27:43].

> "That is all in the harness and the tool calls, and Runvil enables that."
> [27:43]

Today the same learning program is "basically just a prompt that you run in Cursor or Claude Code," which means it is just typing. It does not have to be [27:13], because with Runvil you could create a harness around it connected to a web server [27:13].

### 3.6 Scope: what the company will not work on

*Mateo Robles asked how much of the business would be consulting or process improvement, and whether the plan includes tools that let an enterprise distribute the captured knowledge through software.* Vinnie's answer redirected the question to the pipeline's endpoint: the pipeline starts with capture and **ends when the knowledge is transferred to another person**. The transmission is the product, so yes, distribution through software is in scope, but it is the learning program that does it, not an enterprise workflow [26:12].

*Mateo then asked whether enterprises with confidentiality and privacy constraints would push the service toward self-hosting plus a managed option.* This produced the sharpest scoping statement in the session:

> "I do not want to know about it. I do not want to care about it. I only want us to solve the interesting problem."
> [31:52]

The sequence he sets out:

1. Build the real prototype that works end to end, deployed and really solid.
2. Then bring in an investment partner, who gets a fraction of ownership.
3. That partner brings the people who know how to deploy to the cloud, and the people who solve the privacy problem.

His justification for the deferral is not squeamishness about hard problems. It is a judgment about where his contribution is unique:

> "That problem that you described is not interesting to me at all. It has already been solved a million times, and I have nothing special to add to it, and it is also, quite frankly, busy work. I would rather work on the cool part."
> [32:10]

The cool part, in his words: the pedagogue and the mentograph and the AI talking to you, and eventually an **avatar that talks to you like a person** [32:10], [32:43]. He cites LiveAvatar as evidence the technology is close: you can chat with it and it looks very good, though it is a little choppy over the internet. His forecast is that a year from now virtual avatars will be really good and you will be able to talk to them live [32:43], [33:17].

Mateo's restatement of the answer, which Vinnie confirmed as correct: the value is in the transference from one individual to another individual, not in an implemented enterprise workflow [28:06].

## 4. The pipeline: interview, transcript, extraction, teaching

### 4.1 The four steps

Vinnie states the pipeline as a repeating sequence, and the class performs it weekly:

1. **Interview the subject** who has tacit knowledge [19:10].
2. **Create the transcript** and put it in the `transcripts` directory [19:10].
3. **Run an extraction prompt** that takes the transcript and turns it into something actionable [19:10].
4. **Present it so it can be learned**, eventually through the pedagogue [28:45].

Everything in the class is an instance of this. The session itself is the interview. The transcript is the artifact. The students' extraction prompts are step three.

### 4.2 Tacit knowledge

The thing being captured needs a definition, because it is not in the repository and not in the code:

> "Experiences and skills and judgment that somebody has because they have been doing something for a long time, but they acquired it from experience, and it is not necessarily written down. There are no rules for it."
> [3:29]

This is why reading the source does not substitute for the interview. Vinnie says it directly about his own project: the tacit knowledge is not something you will find by looking in the Rust code, because the code "is just going to overwhelm you" [40:59].

### 4.3 The interviewer's skill, and the mentograph

The premise Vinnie takes from the SAGE paper (*Saving All Gathered Expertise*) is that there are two people in the room: the person who has the knowledge, and the interviewer. The interviewer needs a skill, and the skill is not questioning in general. It is:

- get them to talk about the subject,
- guide them to the subject,
- try to get them to tell a **story** [34:15].

That is the job of the **mentograph**, and Vinnie describes its operation as a sequence:

1. **Chat openly.** You start chatting. The tool is trying to find a story, trying to get you to lock onto a story [34:46].
2. **Lock onto a story.** As soon as it finds one, it performs what is called a **story walk** [34:46].
3. **Story walk.** It gets you to talk about it, and starts asking questions about the story [34:46].
4. **Wait for the energy to come down.** When the story runs out, when the energy comes down, the walk is over [35:16].
5. **Variations and enrichment.** These follow the walk [35:16].
6. **Mutation.** The AI asks what if: "what if you had made a different choice" [35:16].

Why the counterfactuals matter is the single most important technical claim in this section:

> "That exploration of those what-ifs, and those modifications to the story, is the most revealing aspect, because it explains how the person thinks."
> [35:16]

There are, in his count, **seven ways** the tool can modify your story and ask a question that makes you think about it from a different angle. He calls that capture super valuable [35:40].

### 4.4 Talktron, mentograph, and what the prototypes are for

Vinnie names two interviewer artifacts and treats their relationship loosely, which is worth recording rather than resolving:

- **Talktron** is the interview tool he created: it interviews you, creates a transcript, and gets you to talk [5:16].
- **mentograph** is the tool that Will Pak is porting, and Vinnie says "that is pretty much what Talktron is" [23:09].
- The mentograph is also what he calls the tool with the story walk and the seven variations [34:15], [35:40].

The point of the prototypes in his account is not polish. It is that they are a **proof of concept** that can be turned into something real [5:16]. The class is the mechanism by which the crude versions become real ones.

### 4.5 Why extraction is not summarisation

The scribe tool already turns a set of minutes into a document, and some of the class has seen it [19:29]. Vinnie's reason for not stopping there is the sharpest distinction in the session:

> "This just tells you a summary of the conversation. What we really want is a prompt that will extract the knowledge."
> [19:46]

His illustration of the difference is concrete, and he uses his own phrasing to make the failure obvious: "the instructor wants daily results in the repository." That sentence is a statement. It is not knowledge. Nobody cares about it, and it is not something the class is teaching. What an extraction prompt should produce is the thing worth teaching [19:46].

### 4.6 The case study is the class

The research basis for the company is not a separate study, and this is what he called his realisation of the morning:

> "What we learn about how you learn, what works for you, what does not work for you, we are going to record all that, and that is going to become the case study, that is going to become our research that is going to back the product."
> [29:15]

One consequence he draws directly is that the team meeting without him is part of the method (section 5.3): what the team reports about its own learning is the research material.

### 4.7 Where the knowledge lives

*Jonathan Elbaz asked where the knowledge capture will be stored in Runvil Classroom, and how it will be viewed.* Vinnie's answer was, in sequence: Markdown, because everything is Markdown; a folder will be added, because adding folders is free; and then a restatement of his role in the project:

> "I am not pretending to have all the answers. What we are doing is an exploration that we are performing together. Do not think I have the answers to everything, because I do not. If I did, I would just say it. But I need your help exploring this space, because the problem is bigger than one person. That is why I put together this team."
> [30:51]

The substance for the reader: the storage format is decided (Markdown), the location is not yet decided (a folder to be added), and the fact that there is no folder today is not an oversight [30:24], [30:35].

## 5. How the class works

### 5.1 The format is an experiment, and Vinnie says so

> "This is a new idea. I am not going to pretend that I know what I am doing. If you have input, if you would like to learn a different way, or if you think you have some suggestions or some advice, of course I am happy to hear it."
> [17:03]

The first principle of the format is stated as a principle, so that it can be held to:

> "My principle is I want to try to record everything in a transcript."
> [17:20]

### 5.2 The session loop

1. Every session is recorded, transcribed, and placed in the `transcripts` directory [17:20].
2. Everyone can read it. AGENTS.md cleans it up, for profanity and the like [17:20].
3. The transcript is turned into minutes. His worked example is the earlier conversation with Cynthia Prevatte: two participants, and minutes with the seeds and the three planned products in them [17:38], [17:55].
4. The session itself has a shape: Vinnie talks about something, the class asks questions, and all of it goes into the transcript [17:55].
5. Afterwards, the transcripts are **mined for information**, and that becomes documentation for the system [17:55].

The questions are not incidental to the method. They are the input to the next session, which is what the lesson plan's session 2 topics row is for.

The length is bounded on purpose. Vinnie wants a normal high school class: fifty minutes and the bell [41:16]. Cynthia Prevatte tracked the time, and he called assignments at the five minute mark [41:33], [41:42].

### 5.3 Two kinds of meeting

The format has a division of authority that Vinnie asks to be taken literally:

> "There are the meetings that I am in, and then there are the meetings where I am not in it."
> [24:51]

In the second kind, the team gets together without him influencing them, to talk about what worked and what did not. Critique of him is explicitly permitted, on the grounds that he has a thick skin and it is better to blow some steam [24:51]. The reason this belongs in the method, rather than in team morale, is section 4.6: what the team reports about its own learning is the research.

### 5.4 The repository and the reading

The class has a home: the **Runvil Classroom** repository [2:46]. Vinnie's description of the approach is that it will have more documentation, more artifacts, and more participation than his usual way of bringing a team up to speed, and it depends on mutual help: "help me help you, and help you help me" [2:46].

The reading (assigned before class, and Greg Kaleka said he did not have time; Vinnie's response was that reading it all is not realistic [1:20], [1:25]):

| Document | What Vinnie says it is |
|---|---|
| [SAGE: Saving All Gathered Expertise](https://isocpp.org/files/papers/P4046R0.pdf) | A paper about how to interview people to get them to tell stories, and capture tacit knowledge. It describes a system for automating that capture with interviews [3:08], [3:51]. |
| [How an Agent Harness Works](../../../tutorials/tutorial-agent-harnesses.md) | Specially designed to tell you the nuts and bolts of the parts of an agent harness that Runvil cares about. Curated so that once you understand it you understand how every agent harness works: Cursor, Claude Code, ChatGPT web, or whatever [3:51], [4:09]. |
| [Runvil vision](../../../runvil-vision.md) | Explains the prompting language: why do we have it, what is it for, why is it better [4:27]. |
| [Workshop vision](../../../workshop-vision.md) | Self-explanatory: it explains Runvil Workshop, the IDE [4:27]. |
| [Knowledge vision](../../../knowledge-vision.md) | The thing he is telling the class about: what comes after Runvil, and what they are working towards [4:27], [4:48]. |

### 5.5 The other repository: Runvil design

There is a second repository the class needs to know about, **Runvil design**, where Vinnie dumps his reports [35:57]. Three things matter about it:

- **Some of it is out of date.** There is "crap from the history" in it [36:13].
- **Recency is the ranking function.** The newer it is, the better. Last week beats July [36:13], [36:33].
- **`research` is the newest directory**, and it is how Runvil was built in the first place.

Vinnie's account of that directory is also the account of how he learned his own subject. Before Runvil, he did not know how agent harnesses work; he just knew how to type in prompts. So he wrote a prompt that goes in and produces one of these reports, and that is how he learned [37:02]. The example he points to, which Jason Brazeal would recognise, is research into presenting a prompt as an MCP tool [36:33].

The practical instruction for a student is therefore: when you need to know how something works, read `research`, and read the newest thing first.

## 6. The assignments

Vinnie gives the assignments at the five minute mark [41:42], and then repeats them as the closing instruction [44:58]. Combined, they are three, and one of them has a rule he is emphatic about.

### 6.1 Write your own extraction prompt

Everyone participating writes their own prompt that processes the session material and produces a design document their own way, with whatever they think is important in it [20:09]. Then the specific brief:

> "I would like each of you who is participating in the class, after this session, to make a prompt that turns the transcript into design principles."
> [20:36]

He acknowledges the obvious difficulty and answers it with sequencing rather than with a specification:

> "I have not given you any design principles yet, but I will, and then you will look at it, you will adjust your prompt, you will get it to how you want it as best as you can, and do not waste too much time on it."
> [20:36]

So the first version is written against an unknown target, on purpose. The prompt is expected to be revised once the target is shown.

The mechanics, stated twice, are:

1. Cynthia Prevatte puts the transcript in place [45:15].
2. You write a prompt in your own student folder [20:09], [45:15].

### 6.3 Work in small weekly increments, and report what is missing

The ports are a **long term project**. Every week you do a little bit. You are not going to write the whole thing at once [23:45].

What the port produces, besides a Runvil prompt, is a defect list for Runvil itself:

> "You will probably get to a certain point and then there will be a feature that is missing in Runvil, and then that will guide us and we will say, well, maybe we will prioritize that missing feature."

If a workaround exists, it may be good enough; if a feature is added, the prompt gets better, and the port continues [24:13]. ### 6.5 How the work will be judged

*Jason Brazeal asked what the evaluation criteria are, and how the class will know a good job was done.* Vinnie's answer is a refusal to invent criteria in advance:

> "I have no idea. We will look at them together and we will comment on what looks good or what does not, and we will try to figure it out, because I do not know."
> [45:49]

Two things stand in for criteria in the meantime:

- **The scribe baseline.** He will run scribe, and the class can compare against it. His expectation is that the students can beat it, and his reason is a design insight rather than a confidence in effort: a hand-written extraction can be **specific**, for example by putting everything about Runvil first, which scribe does not do [46:06].
- **Iteration.** "It will get better each time." After several rounds, he expects good prompts, and he is willing to say in advance that he does not know what to extract or how to present it [20:59], [47:11].

The standing criterion that he does state is pedagogical:

> "At all times, our goal will be to present the information in a way where people can learn it."
> [20:59]

### 6.6 The problem the assignments are all instances of

Vinnie closes by naming the general problem, which is also the company's core problem, so that the class knows what it is actually practising:

> "Given a transcript which represents an interview, how do we extract the most important information and store it in a way that can be taught? That is the core problem, and you are all going to try to solve it your own way."
> [46:53]

The class is additionally, in his words, a place to "learn how to write reusable prompts," and a group experience in finding out what works [46:37], [47:11].
