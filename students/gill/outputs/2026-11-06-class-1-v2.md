## In one sentence

Falco is selling the interview itself, and he names the whole company's question in the first hour of class: "how do I transfer that knowledge that I have over to you all" [29:33].

## Summary

He arrived at that question as a realisation that morning, and it reorders everything around it: the three products, the prompting language, the IDE, and the class itself. The class is the company's process, run on him first, because his own design decisions were made at 2 a.m. and "They're not written down, and we wanna know what they are" [18:39].

What he refused is a longer list than what he proposed: no summarization where extraction is wanted, no AI-written prompts, no review gate, no confidentiality architecture, no simulation of a person, no IDE owned by someone else. On the enterprise question he is flat: "I don't want to care about it" [31:52].

What is most at risk of being lost is the reasoning behind the artifacts, not the artifacts. The seven story variations are named and never listed, and "These are the seven ways that it can modify your story" [35:40] is the whole of what he says about them.

## 1. Finish at transfer, not at storage

When a capture is finished, transmit it to a person, because "The whole point of capturing the knowledge is so that you can transmit it to another individual" [26:12].

When you plan for knowledge that lives in people, plan for its loss, because "This happens in every organization where there's 2 people or more" [26:47].

When you design a class or a pilot, run it as the product rather than as preparation for it, because the pipeline he means to sell "is actually the process that we need to use for Runvil" [18:19].

When the decisions you need were made by one person, interview that person, because "They're not written down, and we wanna know what they are" [18:39]. The cost he names is that nobody can tell whether an implementation is right or one is missing [18:39].

When students learn something about their own learning, record it, because "that's gonna become our research that's gonna back the product" [29:15].

## 2. Extract, do not summarize

When you need knowledge from a session, write an extraction prompt rather than minutes, because minutes are a summary and not the knowledge: "What we really want is a prompt that will extract the knowledge" [19:46].

When a line in the output is merely a fact about the session, cut it, because "that's not something that we're teaching, that's just a statement" [19:46].

When you rank extracted material, put the speaker's own project first, because that is how you beat a generic tool: "anything about Runvil, put that first" [46:06].

When you choose a store for captured knowledge, choose plain text: "it's all Markdown. Everything's Markdown" [30:24].

## 3. Write the prompt by hand and merge it unreviewed

When you write your extraction prompt, write it by hand, because "if you use AI you're not gonna learn anything" [23:45].

When students disagree about what matters, let them, because the point is to see whose prompts prove more useful: "create the design document their way, whatever they think is important" [20:09].

When your prompt is ready, merge it yourself, because "I don't want to be sitting there having to review anything" [20:59].

When you feed the prompt, feed it the transcript alone, because the run must not get long: "just the transcript is fine" [46:37].

When you merge, avoid the command line and the GitHub page: "don't merge it with the command line, don't merge with the GitHub interface" [45:33].

## 4. Refuse what is already solved, and sequence the rest

When an enterprise asks about confidentiality or self-hosting, decline for now, because "it's already been solved a million times" [32:10].

When the prototype is not yet deployed, defer deployment to a partner, because "When we have the real prototype that works end to end, that's deployed and really solid, then I will bring in an investment partner" [31:52].

When asked what the company will always give away, name the language and the IDE: "Runvil and Runvil Workshop will always be open source" [29:51].

## 5. Get to a story, walk it, then vary it

When you interview someone about their work, get them onto a story: "you guide them to the subject, and you try to get them to tell a story" [34:15].

When the story runs out of energy, spend what is left on variations, because "it explains how the person thinks" [35:16].

When you plan a story tool, expect seven ways to vary it: "These are the seven ways that it can modify your story" [35:40].

When you want someone's judgement, do not go looking for it in their code, because "that's just gonna overwhelm you" [40:59].

## 6. The prompt author assigns authority

When you write a prompt, assign authority deliberately, because "the author of the prompt decides which aspect has the authority" [11:29].

When you add a classifier, delegate to it only by choice, because "you're not forced to do that" [12:05].

When you offer tools, scope them per section, because a capability offered early need not be offered late: "The author can offer tools to the model selectively" [12:05].

## 7. Record every run so a change is cheap to test

When a run is expensive, record it, because "it records everything that it does" [15:21].

When one step of a pipeline is bad, re-run that step alone on the same data, because the earlier steps "could take 2 hours" [16:15].

When the conversation compacts, decide per prompt what stays, because "It all depends on the prompt" [14:21].

## 8. Translate vendor differences in the Gateway

When you extend the Gateway's API, add just enough, because "other apps who use the Gateway won't be bothered" [40:04].

When two vendors differ, absorb the difference in the Gateway, because the frontier models reward their own APIs: "it will translate that into whatever Anthropic wants" [38:15].

When you pass thinking blocks back, never reorder or remove them, because "that would break the chain" and the request is rejected [39:08].

When someone asks where the protocol is specified, point at the document: "It would be in this file, you have to look in this file" [40:50].

## 9. Own the IDE, or its owner decides for you

When you choose an IDE, prefer one you own, because "the people who own it, they like making decisions for me" [13:47].

When you enrich context, do it in your own process, because "I want to enrich the content my way" [13:47].

When you build the IDE, build it around the language, because "The point of the IDE is to have something that understands Runvil" [14:51].

## 10. Run the class on attention, not coverage

When you open a session, lead with the most interesting thing, because that is what gets people interested: "start from the most exciting thing, and I'm gonna work backwards" [2:28].

When you teach one harness, teach the general case, because it then covers every other one: "Once you understand this, you'll understand how every agent harness works" [4:09].

When the session approaches an hour, stop, because "that could be too much for everyone" [41:16].

When you want candour, leave the room, because people speak "without me influencing you" [24:51].

When a student fails two or three times in a row, back off to something simpler, because they need to "build confidence" [7:33].

## 11. The products he will and will not build

When you build a persona of a person, build a confidant and not a clone, because "a virtual simulation of the person, that would be weird" [8:43].

When you design ratings for the marketplace, make them random rather than voluntary, because "they can't be gamed because they're random" [10:42].

When a learner wants the next section, gate it on a quiz: "Quiz him in order to move to the next section" [27:43].

When you plan for talking avatars, plan for a year: "those virtual avatars will be really good" [33:17].

When you are asked for an evaluation criterion, decline to invent one: "I have no idea" [45:49].
